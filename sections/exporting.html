Exporting Your Beat

The export dialog is where a lot of good beats quietly get ruined — wrong bit depth, dithering applied twice, reverb tails chopped off, an MP3 sent to a mix engineer. None of it is complicated once you know what each setting actually does. This section covers getting the file out of FL correctly, and the next two cover splitting it into stems and handing it to someone else.

The Dialog

FL STUDIO'S EXPORT SETTINGS

File → Export → Wave file (or Ctrl + R). These are the settings that actually matter, using FL's own names for them.

▸
Format. WAV for anything that will be worked on further. MP3 only for previews and sharing. FL also offers OGG, FLAC, MIDI and M4A, but WAV and MP3 cover 99% of what a producer needs.
▸
Bit depth. Your three options are 16-Bit integer, 24-Bit integer, and 32-Bit floating point. Use 24-bit for anything going to another human being — artists, mix engineers, stems, your own archive. 16-bit is only for the final file going to a distributor. 32-bit float is for when you want an exact, lossless capture of the project including anything above 0dBFS.
▸
Sample rate isn't in this dialog. It's set in Audio Settings and applies to the whole project. 44.1kHz is the standard for music and is what distributors expect. 48kHz is standard for video. Pick one at the start of a project and don't change it mid-way.
▸
Dithering. Only applies when you render to 16-bit. Leave it off for 24-bit exports and stems. See the dithering card below — the rule is "once, at the very end, only when going down to 16-bit."
▸
Leave remainder. Extends the render past the last bar so decaying sounds — reverb tails, long 808s, delay repeats — finish naturally. This is the one you want almost every time.
▸
Cut remainder. Stops the render dead at the end of the last bar or time marker. Use it when you need an exact bar-length loop. Use it by accident and your beat ends with the reverb sliced off.
▸
Wrap remainder. Takes the decaying tail and folds it back onto the start of the file. Only useful for seamless loops — never for a finished track.
▸
HQ for all plugins. Switches FL's native plugins into their high-quality mode for the render. Slower export, better result. Turn it on for final exports; leave it off for quick reference bounces.
▸
Resampling. The interpolation quality used when pitching samples. Higher Sinc settings sound cleaner on heavily pitched material and cost render time. 24-point Sinc is a sensible final-export setting.
⚠ Export settings are saved per project, not globally. If you set up a perfect export once, save that project as a template — see Template projects in FL Studio Tips & Tricks.

Pre-Flight

BEFORE YOU HIT EXPORT

▸
Check the master isn't clipping. Limiter ceiling at -1dBTP. If the master meter is hitting red anywhere in the track, fix it now — you cannot un-clip an exported file. See Mastering.
▸
Un-solo and un-mute everything. The single most common export mistake: bouncing a beat with a track still soloed from an hour ago, or a muted element you forgot about. Look at the whole mixer before you render.
▸
Set the song length properly. Make sure the Playlist selection or time marker covers the entire arrangement. A stray empty pattern clip at bar 200 gives you a beat with three minutes of silence stapled to the end.
▸
Listen to the first second and the last five. A clipped first transient or a chopped reverb tail is invisible in the project and obvious in the file.
▸
Play the exported file, not the project. Open the rendered WAV in a media player and listen all the way through at least once. Plugins that misbehave only on render — and it happens — are caught here or not at all.
▸
Save the project first. Ctrl + Shift + S. If the export reveals a problem you want the exact session that produced it.
The Technical Bit

BIT DEPTH, SAMPLE RATE & DITHERING

▸
Sample rate = how often the waveform is measured. 44,100 times a second at 44.1kHz. It sets the highest frequency that can be represented — roughly half the sample rate, so ~22kHz at 44.1kHz, which is already above human hearing. Higher sample rates mostly buy you cleaner plugin processing, not audibly more "detail."
▸
Bit depth = how precisely each measurement is stored. It sets the noise floor and therefore the usable dynamic range. 16-bit gives about 96dB, 24-bit about 144dB. That extra room is why you record and exchange files at 24-bit: you can track conservatively with plenty of headroom and still be nowhere near the noise floor.
▸
32-bit float is a different animal. It stores level as a floating point number, so it can hold values above 0dBFS without clipping — you can turn a "clipped" 32-bit float file back down and recover it. Useful for archiving a mix exactly as the project produced it. Not what you send anyone, and never dither it.
▸
What dithering actually does. Going from 24-bit to 16-bit throws away the bottom 8 bits. Without dither, the quietest material — reverb tails, fade-outs — doesn't fade smoothly, it degrades into harsh quantisation distortion. Dither adds a tiny amount of noise that converts that ugly distortion into a barely-audible hiss. You are trading a nasty artefact for a benign one.
▸
The dithering rule. Apply it once, on the final render, and only when reducing bit depth to 16-bit. Not on stems. Not on 24-bit exports. Not on a file that will be processed again afterwards. Dithering twice stacks two layers of noise for no benefit.
▸
Don't convert sample rates repeatedly. Each conversion is a resampling process. Choose 44.1kHz at the start of the project, keep every sample and export at it, and you never think about it again.
Stems & Track-Outs
Terminology

STEMS VS TRACK-OUTS

▸
Stems — grouped bounces. Drums as one file, bass as one file, melodies as one file, FX as one file. Usually four to eight files total. This is what you hand a mastering engineer or use for a live set.
▸
Track-outs (multitracks) — every single element as its own file. Kick, snare, hats, 808, lead, pad, each vocal take. Twenty to sixty files is normal. This is what a mix engineer needs, and what "trackout lease" means on a beat store.
▸
In practice the words get used interchangeably. Producers say "stems" for both. When someone asks for stems, ask which they mean — sending eight grouped files to an engineer expecting forty individual tracks wastes everyone's day.
▸
When in doubt, send track-outs. An engineer can always group your individual files together. They can't separate a kick out of a drum stem.
FL Studio

EXPORTING STEMS IN FL

▸
Tick "Split mixer tracks" in the export dialog. FL renders every mixer track to its own WAV, named after the mixer insert. It doesn't work for MP3, OGG or FLAC — WAV only, which is correct anyway.
▸
This only works if your routing is clean. Every element needs its own mixer insert. Three sounds sharing insert 4 come out as one inseparable file. This is the payoff for the routing discipline in FL Studio Basics — and the reason to name and colour your inserts.
▸
Name your mixer inserts before exporting. The insert name becomes the filename. Do it properly and you get Kick.wav, 808.wav, Lead Pluck.wav. Skip it and you get Insert 7.wav and an engineer who has to open every file to find the snare.
▸
Bypass the master chain first. Your limiter, master compressor and clipper should be off for a stem export. Whoever receives these files is going to do their own master — baked-in limiting just takes options away from them and can't be undone.
▸
Decide about the pre-master bus. Glue compression on the pre-master affects every stem's level relationship. Bypass it for a true track-out. If its character is genuinely part of the sound, leave it and tell the engineer you did.
▸
Watch your sends. Reverb and delay returns export as their own stems. That's usually what you want — the engineer can rebalance the space. Just make sure the return tracks are named clearly (Verb Return, not Insert 22).
Non-Negotiables

THE FOUR STEM RULES

▸
1 — Every stem starts at bar 1. Even if the element doesn't come in until bar 33, the file must start at the very beginning of the song with silence in front. This is what lets someone drag all forty files into a session, line them up at zero, and have everything in sync instantly. Getting this wrong is the number one stem delivery failure.
▸
2 — Every stem is the same length. Same start, same end, all the way through the arrangement. Use "Leave remainder" so tails finish, and make sure every file gets the same treatment.
▸
3 — No master processing, and leave headroom. Individual stems should peak around -6dBFS. Quiet stems are trivially turned up; clipped stems are ruined. Don't push levels "so it sounds good" — the mix engineer sets the balance.
▸
4 — Send the key, BPM and a reference. Include a text file or message with the tempo, the key, and a rough mix of how you imagine it sounding. It takes thirty seconds and it prevents most of the back-and-forth.
⚠ Effects that define a sound should stay printed — a heavily distorted 808, a bit-crushed vocal chop, a filtered pad. Effects that are just mix decisions (broad EQ, bus compression, the master chain) should come off. If you can't tell which category something falls into, print it and mention it.
Packaging

NAMING & FOLDER STRUCTURE

▸
One zip, one clear name. ByrneBeats - Nightdrive - 140bpm - Fmin - TRACKOUTS.zip tells the recipient everything before they've opened anything.
▸
Number the files so they sort usefully. Files sort alphabetically, so prefixing with numbers puts them in a sensible order in the engineer's import window rather than scattering drums between synths.

Avoid spaces and special characters if you can. Underscores and hyphens travel across Windows, Mac and Linux without surprises. Not strictly required any more, but it costs nothing.

Producer Tags

TAGGED VS UNTAGGED

▸
What a producer tag is. A short vocal or sound stamp — your name, a phrase, a signature noise — dropped near the start of a beat and often repeated. It's branding and it's theft protection at the same time.
▸
Tag the preview, not the purchase. The version streaming on your store, YouTube or Instagram should be tagged, usually several times through the beat so it can't be cut out. Anything a customer actually pays for is delivered clean.
▸
Scatter tags irregularly. A tag only at 0:04 gets trimmed off in ten seconds. Tags at unpredictable points — mid-phrase, under the hook, over a transition — make a stolen preview unusable.
▸
Keep the tag out of the way musically. Tune it to the key or make it percussive, sit it around -12dBFS, and place it where the arrangement has space. A tag that buries the melody costs you sales.
▸
Export both versions at once. Render tagged and untagged in the same session so they're bit-identical apart from the tag. Name them unambiguously — sending a tagged file to a paying customer is an avoidable, embarrassing mistake.
To an Artist

SENDING A BEAT TO AN ARTIST

▸
Send the WAV, and an MP3 too. The WAV is what they record against. The MP3 is what they'll actually listen to on their phone deciding whether they like it. Give them both and remove the friction.
▸
Put the BPM and key in the filename. Nightdrive_140bpm_Fmin.wav. Artists and their engineers need both constantly, and the filename is the one place the information can't get lost.
▸
Leave headroom on a beat that will have vocals on it. If you've mastered the beat to full commercial loudness, there's no room left for a vocal to sit on top without the whole thing being re-limited. Peaks around -6dBFS and a lighter master chain gives the mix engineer somewhere to work.
▸
Consider an intro count-in or a clean 4 bars. Some artists find it much easier to come in on a beat that gives them a bar or two of space at the top.
▸
Agree the terms before they record. A conversation about splits and licensing after an artist has already written a hook to your beat is a much harder conversation. Sort it up front, in writing, however informal.
To an Engineer

SENDING TO A MIX ENGINEER

▸
Track-outs, 24-bit, all starting at bar 1. The four stem rules above are the whole job. Get those right and you are already an easier client than most.
▸
Include the rough mix. Non-negotiable. It communicates intent faster than any email.
▸
Include a reference track. A commercial record that sounds like the target. "Make it sound like this" is genuinely useful direction; "make it sound better" is not.
▸
Say what's deliberate. If the 808 is meant to be that distorted, or the hats are meant to be that quiet, say so. Otherwise a good engineer will "fix" it.
▸
Don't send the FLP. Unless they've asked for it and also use FL, a project file is useless to them — they won't have your plugins or samples. Audio files are the universal format.
▸
Send one link, not thirty attachments. A single zip on a file host. Email attachment limits will otherwise turn a two-minute job into a day of back-and-forth.
Checklist

THE DELIVERY CHECKLIST

▸
Nothing soloed, nothing accidentally muted.
▸
Master isn't clipping — ceiling at -1dBTP.
▸
Correct bit depth for the destination (24-bit unless it's a distributor).
▸
Dither only if rendering to 16-bit, and only once.
▸
"Leave remainder" on, so tails finish.
▸
Filename carries the title, BPM and key.
▸
Stems all start at bar 1 and run the same length.
▸
Tagged and untagged versions are clearly distinguishable.
▸
Rough mix MP3 included.
▸
You have listened to the exported file end to end.
The whole checklist takes about five minutes. Skipping it is how a beat gets returned, a deadline gets missed, or a customer gets a file with your tag all over it.
