Compression
Foundation

HOW COMPRESSION WORKS

▸
Threshold: The level where compression kicks in. Lower = more compression engaged. Aim for -18 to -24dB on drums to get meaningful gain reduction.
▸
Ratio: How hard it clamps above threshold. 2:1 = gentle glue. 4:1 = moderate. 8:1+ = heavy. Limiting = ∞:1.
▸
Attack: How fast compression reacts. Slow attack (30–100ms) lets transients through for punch. Fast attack (1–5ms) tames peaks, good for controlling sibilance or harsh transients.
▸
Release: How fast compression lets go. Too fast = pumping. Too slow = squashed sustain. Match release to the tempo and groove of the track.
▸
Make-up gain: Compensate for gain reduction. Always gain-match before A/B testing — louder almost always sounds better regardless of quality.
Drums: 4:1–8:1
Bus: 2:1–4:1
Parallel crush: 8:1–∞
Use Cases

COMPRESSION TYPES

2:1
Glue, bus, gentle shaping
4:1
Drums, standard control
8:1
Aggressive, parallel crush
∞:1
Limiting, brick wall
▸
Serial: One compressor in the signal path. Simpler. Can kill transients if pushed too hard.
▸
Parallel: Compressed signal blended with dry. Preserves transients, adds body and sustain. Preferred for sampled drums.
▸
Sidechain: External signal triggers compression. Classic pump: kick triggers compression on bass or pad.
FL Studio / Settings

COMPRESSOR SETTINGS

▸
Fruity Compressor — preferred for drum bus. Simple, transparent. Use when you want clean gain reduction without coloration or multiband phase issues.
▸
Fruity Limiter (comp mode) — versatile. Set threshold low enough (~-18 to -24dB) to actually engage the compressor. The waveform display shows output level, not gain reduction — you can't read GR directly from it.
▸
kHs Compressor — part of the free Kilohearts Essentials pack. May have better visual feedback than Fruity options. Worth exploring for drum bus or pre-master work.
▸
Maximus — avoid on drum buses. Multiband introduces phase artifacts. Reserve for specific multiband tasks, not general compression.
▸
Judging GR without a meter: Bypass compressor and compensate with make-up gain until levels match. The difference between where the gain was vs. where you had to boost it back = roughly how much GR is happening.
EQ & Frequency
Reference

FREQUENCY SPECTRUM MAP

EQ Principles

GENERAL EQ RULES

▸
Cut before you boost. Removing problem frequencies makes room. Boosting on top of mud just makes more mud.
▸
High-pass everything that doesn't need sub. Pads, guitars, vocals — clean below 80Hz. Even tiny cuts clear meaningful headroom.
▸
Narrow cuts, wide boosts. Surgical narrow Q for problem frequencies. Wide shelves or gentle bells for adding character.
▸
Always EQ in context. Solo EQing is deceptive. What sounds right soloed often sounds wrong in the full mix.
▸
Don't over-boost. More than +4–6dB with a narrow bell usually means the sample needs replacing, not more EQ.
Drum Bus EQ

DRUM BUS EQ

▸
High-pass the Drum Crush track at 80–120Hz. The main bus handles the low end — the crush track only needs mids and up for body and energy.
▸
Tame harsh highs on the crush track (8–12kHz shelf cut). Heavily compressed hi-hats get brittle.
▸
Boost mids (1–3kHz) on the crush track for extra snare body and aggression to blend back in.
▸
High-pass reverb returns at 100–200Hz. Low-end reverb = mud, not space.
Mixing Fundamentals
Fundamentals

MIX FUNDAMENTALS

▸
Gain stage before anything else. Individual tracks peaking around -12 to -18dBFS. Headroom first, then mix.
▸
Mix in mono first. If it doesn't work in mono, stereo width is masking problems. Fix translation before adding width.
▸
Kick and bass are the foundation. Balance these two first. Everything else sits around them.
▸
Reference constantly. Your ears adjust to your own mix. Comparing against a commercial track recalibrates them.
▸
Take breaks. 20–30 min away resets ear fatigue. Decisions on fresh ears are almost always better.
Faders & Sends

FADER DISCIPLINE

▸
Blend with faders, not send amounts. Sends at 100%, use track faders for blend. Applies to parallel tracks and reverb/delay returns.
▸
Low-level monitoring test. If the mix still sounds balanced at low volume, the frequency balance is solid. Quiet = brutal truth-teller.
▸
Automation over static faders. Even subtle fader moves during transitions add energy and dimension a static mix can't have.
Reverb & Delay

REVERB & DELAY

▸
Send/return setup. Reverbs and delays on dedicated return tracks. Multiple elements feeding the same space = coherent room glue.
▸
High-pass reverb returns below 100–200Hz. Low-end reverb = mud.
▸
Pre-delay (10–25ms) separates the dry source from the reverb tail. Maintains clarity while keeping the effect present.
▸
Delay throws for variation. Automated delay on a single word or snare hit adds interest without a constant wet effect.
Stereo Field

PAN & WIDTH

▸
Kick and bass: center. Low frequencies lose energy and cause phase issues when panned.
▸
Hi-hats can be panned subtly (L or R 20–40%) to open up the center for main elements.
▸
Wide pads/chords fill the sides. If your melody is centered, widen chords to fill the stereo field.
▸
Haas effect (10–40ms delay on one side) creates width without pitch issues — but always check mono compatibility.
Depth & Dimension
The Concept

DEPTH IS THE THIRD DIMENSION

▸
Panning is left-to-right. Depth is front-to-back. Most producers mix in two dimensions and wonder why the result sounds flat and pasted together. Depth is what turns a flat surface into a room.
▸
Think in four zones. Zone 1 is right in your face, zone 4 is far back in the room. If every element sits in zone 1 you get a wall. If everything sits in zone 4 you get fog. A mix reads as 3D when elements are distributed across the zones.
▸
Placement follows perceived loudness, not the meter. A sound's apparent distance comes from how loud it feels — its density, brightness and attack — not the number on the fader. Two tracks peaking identically can sit in completely different zones.
▸
How many zones you use is a genre call. Trap and drill live mostly in zones 1–2 with a deliberate ambient bed behind. Cinematic and R&B spread across all four.
Z1 — in your face
Z2 — present
Z3 — behind
Z4 — far back
Reference

THE FOUR DEPTH ZONES

Nothing here is a rule about which sound goes where — it is about making sure the zones are not all empty except one.
The Four Tools

MOVING A SOUND BACK

▸
1 — Level. The obvious one, and the one that costs you most. Turning something down moves it back but also removes it from the mix. Use the other three first.
▸
2 — Low-pass filter. Close sounds carry more high-frequency content than distant ones — air absorbs highs over distance. Roll the top off a piano and it steps behind the vocal without losing a dB of level. The more you cut, the further away it goes.
▸
3 — Transient. Close sounds have sharp attack; background sounds have soft, slow attack. Kill the attack and the sound retreats. Three ways in: a transient designer set to cut attack, the synth's own amp envelope attack time, or a short fade at the head of the sample.
▸
4 — Reverb. The one everybody reaches for first, and the one that muddies fastest. It works — but it works best after the other three have done their part.
⚠ Level is the blunt instrument. Filter, transient and reverb all move a sound backwards while it keeps its place in the mix — that's why they're the better tools.
Compression for Depth

DEPTH WITHOUT TOUCHING TONE

▸
To push a sound back: fast attack, fast release, threshold set so the compressor catches the initial transient only. Killing that first transient is what moves it away — and how much compression you apply sets how far.
▸
To pull a sound forward: do the opposite — slow attack so the transient punches through, or a transient designer adding attack.
▸
Why this beats EQ sometimes: a low-pass darkens the sound as well as moving it. Transient shaping moves it without changing its tone at all. Reach for this when you like how something sounds and just need it out of the way.
Reverb Parameters

DEPTH FROM THE REVERB ITSELF

▸
Early vs late reflections. Early reflections bounce off one surface before reaching you — they read as close. Late reflections have bounced many times — they read as far. More late = further away.
▸
Dry/wet is the crude distance control. More wet = further back.
▸
Pre-delay is the clever one. It mainly sets how big the room reads — longer pre-delay, bigger space. But it also lets the dry transient arrive first, before the reverb starts. That's how you give a lead vocal a big space and still keep it on top of the mix.
▸
Very short, early reflections only = 3D without wash. A reverb built from early reflections with a very short decay stretches the dry signal and adds dimension you feel rather than hear. This is the trick for rap vocals that need depth but have to stay dry.
The Real Tradeoff

CLARITY COSTS ENERGY

▸
Nobody tells you this part. Filling the 3D space properly — every element with its own spot, like players on a stage — gives you a clean mix. But the more surgical you get, the more energy you give away.
▸
Concretely: pushing synth chords behind the vocals with reverb and a low-pass, or widening them to clear the center, loses the transient and the punch they were contributing in the middle. The mix gets cleaner and the track hits softer.
▸
So decide what the track needs. A dense, aggressive beat wants elements fighting in the center. A spacious, emotional one wants separation. "Clean" is not automatically "better" — it's a choice you make per track.
⚠ If a mix sounds clean but lifeless, you probably over-separated it. Pull one or two elements back toward the center and let them collide a little.
Stereo Width & Mono
Fundamentals

WHERE WIDTH ACTUALLY COMES FROM

▸
Width is a difference between left and right. Identical volume, frequency content and timing in both channels reads as dead center — that's mono. Every widening technique is just a way of introducing a difference.
▸
Volume difference only is still technically mono — it just gets perceived as coming from the louder side. That's all a pan pot does.
▸
Timing difference (Haas) is the strongest and the most dangerous. Under roughly 35ms of offset your ears can't separate the two arrivals, so they read it as width instead of an echo.
▸
Frequency difference is the safest. Two different EQ curves, one per channel, gives real width with no phase risk. If your EQ has no L/R mode, duplicate the track, hard-pan the copies, and EQ each differently.
▸
Performance difference is the most natural of all. Record the same part twice and pan the takes apart — nobody plays a part identically twice, so you get pitch and timing variation for free. This is why doubled guitars and harmonies sound wide with no plugin at all.
Placement

THE FIVE WIDTH ROLES

▸
Mono center — the elements that drive the track and carry the punch. Kick, snare, sub bass. Non-negotiable.
▸
Wide center — primary sounds that lead but still need presence in the middle. Lead vocal, lead melody, the top layer of the bass.
▸
Super wide — background sounds whose job is filling the stereo field, and secondary sounds harmonizing the lead.
▸
Mono panned — underrated in crowded mixes. A mono sound at 40% left is easy for the ear to locate and won't get masked by the stack of wide elements.
▸
Wide panned — secondary sounds in busy sections, when a lot is playing at once.
The Low End Rule

ONLY BELOW 150Hz HAS TO BE MONO

▸
Two reasons, and neither is superstition. First, frequencies that low fill the whole stereo space and the ear can't localise them anyway, so widening them changes almost nothing you can hear. Second, centering them gives the track focus and rules out phase cancellation where it does the most damage.
▸
Above 150Hz is a free choice. Any top bass layer, the mid content of an 808, a grit layer — wide or centered, whichever the track wants. "Keep the bass mono" is a rule about the sub, not the whole bass.
▸
If the sub level drops when you sum to mono, that's not a mastering problem to patch with an imager. Go back and fix the bass sound in the production session.
Mono Compatibility

WHAT SURVIVES THE MONO FOLD

▸
You will lose information in mono. Accept that. The goal isn't preserving the stereo mix — it's making sure the elements that drive the track keep their impact when a phone speaker sums it.
▸
The rule that makes it work: every driving element needs one mono, centered layer somewhere in the stereo mix — and you want a centered element in each frequency range, so the mono playback stays full instead of hollow.
▸
Phase cancellation, plainly: summed to mono, two signals in phase add up — peak meets peak. Fully out of phase they cancel to silence. Partially out of phase you get partial cancellation: quieter, thinner, and you won't know why.
▸
Haas on the lead is a trap. Save time-offset widening for secondary sounds, where a mono fold losing some of it doesn't cost you the hook.
Reference

PHASE, SUMMED TO MONO

This is why a mix can sound huge on headphones and thin on a phone. Check every widening move in mono before you keep it.
Balanced Panning

KEEPING THE FIELD IN BALANCE

▸
Keep at least one centered element covering each main frequency range. That's the spine of the mix.
▸
Pan a rhythmic element right, compensate with another rhythmic element left. Otherwise the groove itself leans to one side.
▸
Pan a mid/high instrument right, compensate with another mid/high left. Balance the frequency content of the two sides, not just the number of tracks.
▸
Result: a solid center with L and R matched in both frequency content and rhythm — which is what "balanced mix" actually means.
M/S & L/R

PROCESSING THE SIDES SEPARATELY

▸
Three modes on most modern plugins. Stereo hits both channels together. L/R lets you process one channel alone. M/S lets you process the center or the sides independently.
▸
L/R is for fixing something you panned. Hi-hats sitting left and getting harsh? Run the EQ in L/R mode and work the left channel only, leaving the right untouched.
▸
M/S is for clearing the center. The move that saves crowded vocal mixes: put a mid/side EQ on the chord instrument, cut 1–3.5kHz on the MID channel, and boost the same band on the SIDES. The chords keep their energy out wide while the center opens up for the vocal.
▸
Multiband imaging shapes the width of one band at a time — width on the highs opens a track up without touching the low end. Always check the result in mono.
Quick Wins

WIDTH TRICKS THAT TAKE 30 SECONDS

▸
Alternating hat width. A mono hat in the center, and a wider hat layered on every other hit. Instant movement in the top end without an auto-pan.
▸
Delay panning. Pan the source one way and its delay return the opposite way. Adds width and saves space in a busy mix — works especially well on guitars.
▸
Auto-pan on rhythmic elements only. Shakers, percussion, arps. Don't run it on more than one or two sounds in the same section or the mix stops sitting still.
▸
Background vocals: pan the high ones wider than the low ones. Low BVs add body underneath the lead without masking it, so they can sit fairly close in. High BVs compete with the lead directly — push them out to the sides.

EQ (corrective): Fix frequency problems that made it through the mix. Small moves — 0.5 to 2dB max. More than that = a mix problem, not a mastering problem.

Loudness & Translation

LOUDNESS TARGETS

Spotify: –14 LUFS
Apple: –16 LUFS
YouTube: –14 LUFS
▸
Mastering too loud backfires. Platforms normalize loudness. Over-limiting to hit –7 LUFS means your track gets turned down AND sounds squashed.
▸
Dynamic range goal: 6–10 DR for most modern beats. Trap often goes lower, acoustic goes higher.
▸
Check on multiple speakers. Laptop, headphones, earbuds, car. If it translates everywhere, it's done.
▸
Mastering can't fix mix problems. Muddy mix = muddy master. A limiter makes a bad mix loud and bad. Fix problems in the mix.
Perceived Loudness
The Core Idea

WHY TWO TRACKS AT 0dB SOUND DIFFERENT

▸
Peak level is not loudness. Two tracks can both peak at exactly 0dB and one still sounds obviously louder. Peak only tells you the single highest point in the file — it says nothing about how the track feels.
▸
Perceived loudness comes from two things. RMS level — the average level over time — and frequency content. That's it.
▸
The flute and the guitar. A soft flute and a heavily distorted guitar can peak identically on the meter. The guitar sounds far louder because of its harmonics, its excited top end and its heavy compression. Same peak, completely different perceived loudness.
▸
This is why saturation makes things louder without moving the fader. Adding harmonic content raises perceived loudness while the peak stays where it was.
Peak = highest point
RMS = average over time
LUFS = perceived loudness
Peak vs RMS

THE TWO METERS THAT MATTER

▸
Peak is the loudest single instant in the signal. A quiet track can still have one spike that hits hard, and the peak meter will report that spike as the whole story.
▸
RMS is the average level measured over a window of time. Two signals peaking at the same level can have very different RMS — the one with the higher RMS is more consistent in volume, and it's the one that sounds louder.
▸
Raising RMS means compressing the dynamic range. That's the whole mechanism. You shrink the distance between the loud and the quiet, the average comes up, and the track reads as louder.
▸
Dynamic range is the emotional relationship between the hard and soft parts of a track — and it's genre-dependent. Classical is wide and dynamic; modern trap is narrow by design. Mastering is a compromise between the dynamics you built in the mix and the RMS you want to reach.
The Distortion Trap

PEAK VS TRUE PEAK

▸
The symptom: you set the master limiter to 0dB, it sounds great in FL, you upload it to SoundCloud or Spotify, and it comes back with distortion you never heard.
▸
The cause is digital-to-analog conversion. The highest point your digital samples reach is the sample peak. The highest point the resulting analog waveform reaches — the curve drawn between those samples — is the true peak, and it sits higher.
▸
So a file at 0dB sample peak clips after conversion. The true peak goes over 0, the converter runs out of headroom, and you get red meters on a file that measured clean.
▸
The fix: switch the master limiter to True Peak mode and set the ceiling to -1dBTP. Some engineers go to -0.5dBTP; -1 is the safe number and matches the target in the Mastering chain above.

Worked Example

WHY OVER-LIMITING ACTUALLY MAKES YOU QUIETER

▸
Set up the experiment. Upload two masters of the same track to Spotify. Version A is dynamic and measures -14 LUFS. Version B is squashed and measures -7 LUFS.
▸
Spotify normalises both to its target. Version A already sits at the target, so it plays back untouched. Version B gets turned down by 7 LUFS to match.
▸
The result is the opposite of what you wanted. Both now play at the same perceived loudness — but version B threw away its peaks to get to -7 and can't get them back. Version A still has its transients. The dynamic master hits harder on the platform than the loud one.
▸
This is what ended the loudness war. Normalisation removed the reward for crushing a master. Chasing -7 LUFS in 2026 means your track gets turned down and arrives squashed.
⚠ You don't need a separate master per platform. Find one target that sits sensibly across the services you release to, and let normalisation do the rest.
Legitimate Loudness

FIVE WAYS TO GET LOUDER HONESTLY

▸
1 — Fill the frequency spectrum. A track with content across the whole range reads as louder at the same meter reading than one with holes in it. Arrangement is a loudness tool.
▸
2 — Sidechain the kick and bass. The low end is what makes the limiter distort first. Removing the clash between kick and bass buys you real dB before distortion.
▸
3 — Saturation. Harmonics raise perceived loudness, and shaving the spikes off transients hands headroom back to the limiter. Two benefits from one plugin.
▸
4 — Fix phase problems. If the main elements lose impact in mono, you're paying for it in loudness everywhere. See Stereo Width & Mono.
▸
5 — Use fewer elements. A less crowded track lets the final limiter go further before it starts distorting. Deleting a layer can be a louder master.
Buying Headroom

WHY THE LIMITER DISTORTS EARLY

▸
Distortion on a master comes from the bass. The low frequencies hit a point where they can't be pushed further without breaking up — and they get there long before anything else does.
▸
Pick short kicks. A long kick tail spends more time occupying the low end, which means more time fighting the limiter.
▸
Pick a bass with top-end content. If the bass has audible harmonics above the sub, you can run its overall level lower and it stays audible on small speakers. Less sub energy, same presence.
▸
Exaggerate the kick/bass sidechain. For loudness purposes, push it to where the bass almost fades out while the kick plays. You can always dial it back if it pumps too obviously.
▸
Two gentle limiters beat one working hard. Put the first in place to tame the loud peaks and even the waveform out, and the second to add the loudness. Serial limiting buys extra input gain before you hear distortion.
Honest A/B

VOLUME MATCH OR YOU'RE FOOLING YOURSELF

▸
Louder always sounds better. Your ears are not neutral about level — a 1dB lift reads as "warmer, fuller, more finished" even when nothing else changed. Every A/B you do without matching level is a lie.
▸
How to do it: note the peak before the plugin — say -9dB — then set the plugin's output gain so it still reads -9dB after. Now bypass and compare.
▸
Do the same for mix vs master. Match the levels before you decide the master is an improvement, or you'll approve anything that's simply louder.
▸
Most compressors have a makeup or output gain for exactly this. Use it as a measuring tool, not just a volume knob.
Soundcheck

WHAT EACH SYSTEM IS ACTUALLY FOR

▸
Don't just "check on other speakers" — check for something specific on each. Every system answers a different question.
▸
Headphones: detailed mids and highs in an isolated environment. This is where you catch harshness and sibilance.
▸
Car: is the bass destroying everything above it? Cars flatter low end more than any other system — if the mix survives a car, the low end is under control.
▸
Phone and laptop: do the kick and bass carry enough top information to still be audible with no sub at all?
▸
Mono wireless speaker: do the main elements keep their impact when everything is summed?
▸
Two listening levels, always. One loud enough to actually hear the bass, and one quiet enough to hold a conversation over. The quiet one is easier on your ears and shows you the mids and highs honestly.
